These are mistakes that come up again and again in mathematics. Most are not caused by difficult mathematics. They happen because a rule is being used in the wrong place, a step is being skipped, or the answer is not being checked.

Use this page as a quick reference when something in your work does not look right.

## Algebra

### 1. Cancelling terms that are being added or subtracted

You can cancel **factors**, not individual terms that are being added or subtracted.

For example, in

$$
\frac{x+3}{x}
$$

you cannot cancel the $x$ in the numerator with the $x$ in the denominator.

Before cancelling, ask: **Are these quantities factors of the entire numerator and denominator?**

### 2. Forgetting to distribute to every term

When multiplying through brackets, the outside factor must multiply **every term** inside the brackets.

$$
3(x+4)=3x+12
$$

A common mistake is to multiply only the first term.

### 3. Losing a negative sign

Negative signs are one of the most common sources of errors.

Be especially careful when:

- subtracting a bracket,
- multiplying or dividing negative numbers,
- moving between lines of work,
- entering expressions into a calculator.

If a negative sign is in front of a bracket, it affects **every term** inside the bracket.

### 4. Combining terms that are not like terms

Only like terms can be combined.

$$
3x+5x=8x
$$

but

$$
3x+5x^2
$$

cannot be simplified by combining the two terms.

The variable part, including the exponent, must match.

## Exponents and Radicals

### 5. Adding exponents when you are adding terms

The exponent rule

$$
x^a x^b=x^{a+b}
$$

applies when you are **multiplying** powers with the same base.

It does not mean that

$$
x^2+x^3=x^5
$$

Addition and multiplication use different rules.

### 6. Distributing an exponent over addition

In general,

$$
(a+b)^2 \neq a^2+b^2
$$

The bracket must be expanded correctly.

For example,

$$
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2
$$

### 7. Forgetting that a square root represents the principal root

$$
\sqrt{25}=5
$$

not $\pm 5$.

However, if you are solving

$$
x^2=25
$$

then the solutions are

$$
x=\pm5
$$

These are two different situations.

## Equations

### 8. Doing something to only one side of an equation

An equation is a statement that two expressions are equal.

Whatever operation you perform on one side must also be performed on the other side.

Think of an equation as a balance: both sides must stay equal.

### 9. Dividing by something that could be zero

Before dividing both sides of an equation by a variable expression, check whether that expression could equal zero.

Dividing by a variable can sometimes remove a valid solution.

### 10. Not checking a solution

A value obtained during algebra is not automatically correct.

Whenever practical, substitute the answer back into the **original equation**. This is especially important with:

- rational equations,
- radical equations,
- absolute-value equations,
- equations where both sides were squared.

## Functions and Graphs

### 11. Mixing up $x$- and $y$-coordinates

Coordinates are always written

$$
(x,y)
$$

The first number tells you the horizontal position. The second tells you the vertical position.

### 12. Mixing up the $x$-intercept and the $y$-intercept

At an $x$-intercept:

$$
y=0
$$

At a $y$-intercept:

$$
x=0
$$

The name tells you which **axis** the graph is crossing, not which variable you set to zero.

### 13. Treating $f(x)$ as multiplication

$f(x)$ means **the value of the function at $x$**.

It does not mean $f\times x$.

If

$$
f(x)=2x+3
$$

then

$$
f(4)=2(4)+3=11
$$

### 14. Ignoring the domain

A formula may produce an algebraic answer that is not allowed in the original problem.

Always check restrictions such as:

- denominator cannot equal zero,
- the expression under an even root cannot be negative when working with real numbers,
- the context of the problem may restrict possible values.

## Trigonometry

### 15. Using the wrong calculator angle mode

Before working with trigonometry, check whether your calculator is in **degrees** or **radians**.

A correct calculation in the wrong angle mode will still give the wrong answer.

### 16. Choosing a trigonometric ratio before identifying the sides

For right-triangle trigonometry, first identify the sides relative to the angle you are using:

- opposite,
- adjacent,
- hypotenuse.

Then choose sine, cosine, or tangent.

Do not choose the ratio first and try to make the sides fit it afterward.

## Calculator and Written Work

### 17. Rounding too early

Keep extra digits during intermediate steps and round only at the end unless the question tells you otherwise.

Rounding too early can make the final answer noticeably inaccurate.

### 18. Trusting the calculator without checking whether the answer makes sense

A calculator will correctly evaluate what you typed, even if you typed the wrong expression.

Before accepting an answer, ask:

- Is the sign reasonable?
- Is the size reasonable?
- Does the graph or context support it?
- Did I enter the brackets correctly?
- Am I in the correct calculator mode?

### 19. Writing only the final answer

In mathematics, the method matters.

Showing your work makes it possible to:

- find mistakes,
- earn partial credit,
- demonstrate understanding,
- check your reasoning later.

A correct answer with no understandable work may not show that you understand the mathematics.

### 20. Continuing after an answer becomes obviously unreasonable

If you get a negative length, a probability greater than 1, an impossible angle, or a value far outside what the graph suggests, stop and investigate.

An unreasonable answer is often a useful warning that something went wrong earlier.

## A Good Final Check

Before submitting your work, ask yourself:

1. Did I answer the question that was actually asked?
2. Did I show enough work for someone else to follow my reasoning?
3. Did I keep track of negative signs and brackets?
4. Did I use the correct formula or rule?
5. Did I round only when appropriate?
6. Does my answer make sense?

Mistakes are part of learning mathematics. The goal is not to never make them. The goal is to **recognize them, understand why they happened, and avoid repeating them**.
